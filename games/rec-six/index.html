How it's drawn

A real 3D gym, squeezed through a few simple steps so it reads as pixel art.

  1. A tiny canvas

    The scene is rendered into a small image, about 480 × 300 pixels, then each pixel is blown up to a 3 × 3 block on your screen. No smoothing, so the edges stay crisp.

  2. A flat, tilted camera

    The camera looks down at about 31° with no perspective, like an architect's drawing. It is nudged so the gym's centre always lands on a whole pixel, which stops edges from shimmering as things move.

  3. A mirror for the floor

    Before the main picture, the whole gym is drawn again upside down. The varnished floor borrows a faint, slightly smeared copy of that image, which gives the soft glossy reflections of players, lamps and windows.

  4. Outlines from depth

    Every pixel knows how far away it is. Where a neighbouring pixel is more than half a metre closer, the farther pixel is darkened, which traces the edge of each player, post and bleacher row.

  5. A fixed box of paints

    Each colour is snapped to the nearest of about 70 hand-picked colours: maple, court-line paint, cinder block, pad blue, team orange and purple. The match is measured in a perceptual colour space so light and dark are judged the way eyes judge them. A 4 × 4 pattern of tiny nudges (dithering) blends neighbouring paints.

  6. Light that behaves

    The sun comes through the high windows past a hidden roof that only exists to cast shadows, so beams land in patches. At night, eight hanging lamps take over and warm up with a flicker when switched on.

  7. Box people

    Every person is about twenty boxes. Each frame their hands and feet get targets (a platform, a setting window, a spike swing) and a two-bone solver bends elbows and knees to reach them.

  8. Players who look ahead

    The ball flies with gravity, drag, float wobble and topspin. Players simulate its flight to decide who can reach it, then aim passes, sets and attacks by testing launch angles until the simulated ball lands on target.
