How it's drawn

Every frame goes through the same few steps.

  1. A real 3D stadium. The track, the jump pits, the stands and about forty athletes and officials are simple boxes in a 3D scene, lit by a sun or by floodlights, with real shadows.
  2. Rendered small on purpose. The camera draws the scene into a tiny picture, about 480 by 300 dots, with no smoothing. Each dot later becomes one chunky pixel on your screen.
  3. A mirror copy for puddles. After rain, the whole scene is drawn a second time upside down. Puddles on the track look into that copy, so you see runners and lights reflected, with ripples when someone splashes through.
  4. Ink lines. The renderer remembers how far away every dot is. Where a dot sits half a metre or more behind its neighbour, it gets darkened, which draws an outline around people, hurdles and bars.
  5. Only 75 paints. Each dot is swapped for the closest colour in a fixed set of track reds, grass greens, sand, mat blues, singlet colours and so on. Closeness is judged the way eyes judge it, with brightness counting a bit more than hue. An optional checkerboard pattern (dithering) blends between paints.
  6. Blown up. The tiny picture is stretched back to your screen with every dot copied as a solid square. The camera is nudged so the stadium always lands on whole dots, which stops shimmering when it moves.

The athletes are posed every frame. Their feet are pinned to the track while they push, so their legs always match their speed. Times come from a small physics model with reaction, acceleration, top speed, fatigue and wind.
